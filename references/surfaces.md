# The surfaces people forget

An inventory built from memory is always short, and it is short in a predictable direction. People remember what they made. They forget what other people put up, and they forget anything they stopped looking at.

Work through this list before calling an inventory complete. Every entry here has shown up as somebody's first impression.

## Owned

They control the server or the account that publishes it.

- **The homepage.** Usually remembered, rarely checked as a crawler sees it
- **Every other page on the site.** About, pricing, contact, careers, legal. The About page is frequently the oldest text on the whole domain
- **Old landing pages from past campaigns.** Still live, still indexed, often still carrying a price or an offer that no longer exists
- **Subdomains.** blog., docs., go., try., status., help., old., staging., www versus bare. Staging environments that got indexed are a recurring embarrassment
- **Previous domains.** Anything owned before a rename, especially if it still resolves rather than redirecting
- **PDFs.** One-pagers, whitepapers, decks, media kits. They rank, they never get updated, and nobody thinks of them as pages
- **The email footer and any automated sequence.** Published text that goes out daily with nobody reading it
- **Repository READMEs.** For anyone technical these rank well and are often the most current writing they have

## Claimed

An account on a platform they do not own. They can edit it. They cannot control how it is displayed, indexed, or whether it stays.

- **Professional network profiles.** Headline, About section, experience entries, and any long-form posts published there. Note that several of these platforms block automated access entirely, so a logged-out stranger and a crawler see different things than the owner does
- **Social profiles, current and abandoned.** The abandoned ones still rank. A dormant account with an old bio is a live surface
- **Code hosting profiles.** Profile README, pinned items, organization pages
- **Video and audio channels.** Channel descriptions outlive the content
- **Newsletter and publishing platform profiles.** Substack, Medium, Ghost, dev.to. Often carry a bio written years before anything else
- **App store listings.** Developer name, description, screenshots, last-updated date
- **Marketplace and directory profiles.** Anywhere a service was listed
- **Community profiles.** Forums, Discord, Slack communities with public archives
- **Event platform profiles.** Meetup, Eventbrite, conference systems

## Third-party

Someone else published it. It cannot be edited or removed directly.

- **Press coverage.** Local outlets especially. These rank well, stay up indefinitely, and frequently carry an old title, an old employer, or a name no longer in use
- **Podcast and webinar appearances.** Show notes pages carry a bio supplied years ago and copied since without review
- **Conference and speaker pages.** Same problem. A bio written for one event gets reused by every event after it
- **Syndicated and scraped copies.** An article republished elsewhere, often outranking the original
- **Data-broker and contact-aggregator listings.** Assembled automatically, frequently wrong, and they rank on a person's name. These commonly assert a former employer or a stale title. They are rarely worth fighting and are worth knowing about
- **Review sites and comparison pages.** For companies, these are often the real first contact
- **Wikipedia, Wikidata and knowledge panels.** Where they exist they dominate
- **Co-authored and guest work.** Published on someone else's domain, under their template, with their bio field
- **Anything a former employer still hosts.** Team pages, author archives, case studies naming them

## Two that get missed every time

**Link preview cards.** What appears when a URL is pasted into a chat app, a social post, or a message. This is a separate surface from the page, built from meta tags, and it is frequently wrong in ways nobody notices because nobody tests it. A card can credit the wrong account, show a placeholder image, or carry a description from a template that was never edited.

**The search result itself.** The title and description a search engine shows is a surface. It is assembled from the page but it is not the page, and it is what the overwhelming majority of people actually read. Many people have never looked at their own result as a piece of copy.

## Scoping

Not everything found belongs in the audit.

Keep in scope anything a reasonable stranger would find while looking into the person or company professionally. Keep out personal accounts, private profiles, and anything clearly separate from the work, unless the user explicitly puts it in scope.

When something sits on the line, surface it as a question rather than auditing it unasked.
